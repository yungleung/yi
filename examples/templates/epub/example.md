# Prologue

For one year I lived with a man who rarely spoke, and for most of that year I interpreted a cat.

This is not a confession, because a confession assumes that the person making it has arrived at
the end of the story and can name what he did. I have not arrived anywhere. I can only return to
the apartment as one returns to a room in a house that has already been sold: opening the same
doors in memory, noticing that the furniture has not moved, and finding that the silence has
changed shape.

Shen was my friend before he was my housemate. We had known each other for several years, always
at a distance that felt comfortable until it was tested. He worked in an office with systems and
deadlines. I had lost my job a few weeks before he offered me the second bedroom. The offer came
in the form of a practical question: how much was I paying for the place where I was then living?
When I told him, he looked at the wall for a moment and said there was a room in his apartment and
that the rent would be smaller. Nothing in his face suggested charity. This mattered to me. I
accepted because the answer was financial, and because I did not want to know what else it might
have been.

He had adopted a cat a few months earlier, when he had been living alone. She was a small female
with a dark tabby coat and a white patch under her chin, and she had already been sterilized by
the time I moved in. Shen described the operation without sentiment, as a sequence of dates,
fees, and instructions from the veterinarian. The shaved place on her belly had begun to grow
back. She did not like having it touched. She did not like many things, but she had learned to
tolerate the apartment, and she had learned to tolerate Shen.

I soon learned to tolerate the silence.

At first it seemed hostile. A person enters a shared home expecting questions: How was your day?
Do you mind if I turn on this lamp? Would you like some rice? Shen asked none of them. He moved
through the apartment as if each question had been answered before it could be formed. He put an
extra pair of chopsticks beside the stove. He left the bathroom light on when I had mentioned,
once, that I disliked waking in the dark. He turned the television down before I had noticed that
the sound was too loud. These were not declarations. They were adjustments, small enough to be
missed and continuous enough to become a kind of speech.

The cat noticed him before I did.

Her name was Yuzu. Shen had chosen it because the cat's forehead had a faint amber mark that he
said resembled a citrus peel. I could not see the resemblance, but the name stayed. Yuzu had a
way of sitting in doorways with her tail wrapped around her feet, neither inside nor outside,
waiting for someone to decide which room she belonged in.

It was easy to call that behaviour mysterious. It is harder, and more useful, to watch what a
household asks an animal to carry.

* * *

By day the apartment belonged to Yuzu and me. Shen left before eight. The door closed with a
modest click, and the rooms changed temperature. I made coffee, opened my computer, searched for
jobs, rewrote letters I did not send, and listened for the cat's paws on the floorboards. She
followed the sun from the bedroom to the living room. When the light reached the couch, she slept
there with one paw covering her face. When I moved to the kitchen, she arrived before I had
opened the refrigerator. When I stayed too long at the desk, she walked across the keyboard once
and then went away, satisfied that the machine had been interrupted.

I had no job and nowhere to be. The days were formless. Yuzu gave them a sequence. She woke in
the early morning, demanded food, inspected the windowsill, slept, demanded food again. Her needs
were simple enough that I could meet them, which made the rest of my life feel temporarily
manageable.

Because there were two of us in the apartment all day, I began to think of us as an isolated
system. Shen entered that system in the evening and left it again in the morning. Yuzu moved
between us. She slept on his chair during the day and on the foot of my bed at night. She
followed him to the bathroom in the morning and me to the kitchen in the afternoon. When he came
home, she waited at the entrance and looked from him to me, as though confirming that the
household had been restored.

This is the first interpretation, and it is too tidy: the cat was a bridge. A bridge is an
object built to connect two shores, but we had made Yuzu carry the traffic as well. We spoke to
her about one another. Shen asked her whether I had eaten. I told her that he had worked late
again. Neither of us asked the other. The cat received the question and returned with no answer,
which was exactly the answer the household preferred.

At the time I believed this was a failure of language. Later I understood that it was a system of
language, complete with grammar and omissions.

The year did not begin with that understanding. It began with the problem of two people trying to
live without asking one another what they needed.

# The Animal Between Us

The apartment had three rooms, a narrow kitchen, and a balcony too small to sit on. Shen used the
second bedroom as a study, although he rarely studied there. I slept in it after he moved his
boxes into the hall. Yuzu inspected every box before it was opened and slept in the largest one
for two days. Shen allowed this. He carried the box into the living room and placed it beside the
couch so that she would not have to choose between her new bed and the place where we gathered.

The gesture was thoughtful. I noticed it because he did not point it out.

Shen was a man of preparatory acts. He bought vegetables in quantities that appeared excessive and
then spent Sunday afternoon washing, chopping, and dividing them into containers. He said he did
not like cooking every day. The result was that I could open the refrigerator at noon and find
food already waiting. He never asked me to eat it. He would sometimes look at the containers when
he came home and see which were empty. That was the entire conversation.

When I stopped leaving the apartment for several days, he came back one evening with a bag of
pears. I disliked pears. He did not know that. He put them on the counter and said the shop had
been selling them cheaply. For three days I did not touch them. On the fourth day he cut one into
pieces, arranged them on a plate, and put the plate beside my computer without a word. I ate the
pears because he had cut them. He watched the plate return to the kitchen. Something in his back
relaxed.

This was not intuition. Intuition is the name people give to an observation after it has already
been made. Shen observed the plate, the closed bedroom door, the hour when I stopped typing, the
number of times I left the apartment. He accumulated these observations until they formed a
picture. Only the action emerged. He was thoughtful in the way a thermostat is thoughtful: not
with an inner monologue, but with a repeated adjustment to conditions.

I asked him about work one evening while he was washing a pot. I was sitting on the floor with
my back against the cabinets and Yuzu was between us, cleaning one paw with the seriousness of a
small machine.

Do you talk to the people at your company? I asked.

He kept washing. Then he said, Not more than I have to.

At lunch?

At lunch I eat.

I laughed, though the answer did not feel funny. The water ran over his hands. He turned the pot
and said, Why do you ask?

I do not know. You seem alone.

I have you here. And the cat.

That was the most direct sentence he had spoken to me in weeks. I looked at Yuzu. She had stopped
cleaning her paw and was watching him. Shen put the pot in the drying rack and turned around.

You mean you are alone, he said.

I did not answer.

He dried his hands. He did not come closer. He did not offer consolation or advice. He stood
against the sink and looked at me with the same expression he used when a machine stopped
working: not disappointed, but attentive.

I cannot say what he understood. I can say what happened next. In the morning he asked whether I
had plans for the following afternoon. I said no. He told me to wear comfortable shoes and, when I
asked where we were going, said that it was not far.

At two o'clock we took a tram to the old market and walked from the river toward the hills. Shen
had planned a route. He did not announce it. We passed a stationer, a shop that sold used
watches, a bakery whose doorway smelled of burnt sugar, and a narrow lane where every second
house had a potted plant on its steps. He bought two pastries, gave me one, and ate his while
walking. At the top of a pedestrian bridge we stopped to look at the water.

This was the small city travel he had proposed. It lasted less than four hours. He had chosen it
for me, because he had understood that the apartment had become a container in which my
loneliness could not be measured, and because he could not say, You have been unhappy. He could
only take me outside and hope that I would notice the city again.

Yuzu was waiting at the door when we returned. Shen put down a paper bag of food before he
removed his coat. The cat circled his legs. For a moment I felt the three of us as one
temporary arrangement, an island of bodies in the hallway.

* * *

The disagreements were not about the cat. At least, they were not only about the cat.

I treated Yuzu like a child. I used a high, gentle voice when I spoke to her. I bought a toy on a
string and moved it across the floor until my shoulder hurt. When she bit my hand, I pulled away
and told her it was not kind. I did not mean that she would understand. I meant that my feelings
had been hurt by something that lived outside the social contract and could not be held
responsible for it.

Playing with her was exhausting. She liked the game beyond its pleasure, the way an animal can
like a thing past the point of liking. She bit quickly and without warning; her pupils widened,
her tail moved in a separate rhythm, and she became less a pet than a force with a destination. I
would play for ten minutes, then fifteen, then discover that my patience had ended before her
appetite had. Eventually I left her to the window and went back to the computer. She slept on the
couch. This was the compromise.

Shen saw the cat differently. He wanted her company, but he did not trust the desire. He said
that an animal that bit should not be treated as a child. He said that if she continued, he
would put her outside and let her learn what the street was. He said this calmly, as a matter of
training. When I objected that she had been adopted, sterilized, and lived indoors all her life,
he replied that those were facts about her situation, not arguments against her learning.

He was not indifferent to her. He was more careful with her than I was. He never stepped over her
without looking. He learned the sound of her claws on different surfaces. He opened the balcony
door only wide enough for her head, and he closed it after her tail had passed. When she was ill
for a day and would not eat, he sat on the floor nearby and moved a bowl toward her every hour
without trying to make her drink. Yet the same man could speak about abandoning her without
changing the pitch of his voice.

I saw this contradiction as a moral defect. Perhaps it was also a fear. To need an animal, he
seemed to believe, was to expose oneself to an animal. The threat was not that she had changed
personality. The threat was that she might not recognize an agreement. That thought made him
angry. It made anger the safest face for a dependence he could not bear to admit.

We argued about it in fragments. Nothing was ever settled. I would pick up the toy. He would say
that play was making her wild. I would say that she was not wild; she was small and bored. He
would say that I expected an animal to reason like a person. I would say that he expected her to
obey like a machine. Then we would stop. One of us would make tea. Yuzu would carry the tension to
the couch and fall asleep with her body between our two chairs.

The worst argument was also about the cat.

She bit me on a Saturday evening. It was not a bad bite by the standards of the household. The
skin broke across the base of my thumb and a bead of blood appeared. I said the word she had
heard before, the high surprised no that did not change anything. Shen was in the kitchen. He
turned at the sound and crossed the room before I had reached the bathroom.

Why do you keep provoking her? he asked.

I was not provoking her. I was playing with her with my hand near her mouth, which is not the same
thing, though the distinction was not one Shen was interested in.

She needs boundaries, he said.

She is a cat.

He looked at the blood on my thumb. I saw his face change. It was not the explosive face of a
man who had lost control in the usual sense. It was much quieter than that. He became still, as if
some internal calculation had been completed and the result had been waiting there all along.

Yuzu was sitting on the rug. She had not run. She was washing her paw with the patience of an
animal who did not understand that an event had taken place. Shen reached down, seized her by the
body, lifted her, and brought her down against the floor.

The sound was a flat sound, too ordinary for what it did. Yuzu lay on the rug for a second that
was not a second. Then she scrambled up and lost control of her bladder. A dark patch spread over
the rug. She ran under the bed.

Shen stood over the rug with his hand still raised. The fury had not left him, but it had nowhere
to go. His eyes were wet. He looked at the urine, then at the raised hand, then at the place under
the bed where the cat had disappeared.

You see? he said. Hard to domesticate.

I said nothing.

The sentence was so obviously a shield that words would have struck it and fallen away. I knew that
he would not listen. I also knew, in the way one knows a thing about oneself before one is willing
to say it, that my silence was not neutral. It was a decision. It made the room smaller. It made
his action the only action, and my observation a kind of permission I had not intended to give.

I got a cloth and cleaned the rug. Shen stood in the kitchen doorway while I did it. Neither of
us called the cat. When I finished, I washed the cloth in the sink and hung it over the tap. The
household resumed the shape of its silence. It was worse now, because the silence had a sound in
it.

At midnight I heard Yuzu come out from under the bed. Her nails were careful on the floor. She
stopped at the edge of the living room. Shen was sitting on the couch without a light on. I
could see them from the hall: the man and the small animal, separated by the width of a rug.

Shen put a bowl of food on the floor and pushed it toward her with his foot. Then he withdrew his
foot and sat back. Yuzu waited. She waited so long that I thought she would return under the bed.
At last she crossed the room, keeping the couch at a distance, and ate.

Shen watched her. He did not touch her. Nothing in his posture asked for forgiveness. He was
making a space in which she could decide whether to come back. Perhaps that was the only apology
he knew how to make. Perhaps it was not an apology at all. I went back to my room and closed the
door, and the sound of my closing it became another thing that had happened in the house.

* * *

In the following days Shen and I were especially polite. He asked whether I wanted tea. I said
please, no, thank you. He left a bowl of soup outside my room. I returned the bowl washed. The cat
kept to the edges of the rooms. When Shen came home, she did not rise to meet him. When I sat at
the computer, she did not cross the keyboard. The three of us had become careful with one
another, which is not the same as gentle.

I wanted to explain to him that violence does not teach an animal the meaning of a rule; it
teaches the animal the location of danger. But I had no confidence that explanation would reach
him, and less confidence that my own tenderness had been as harmless as I wanted it to be. I had
also treated Yuzu as an object of my needs. I had called it love when I wanted her to behave like
a child, and I had withdrawn when she behaved like a cat.

The difference between Shen and me was not that one of us loved her and the other did not. It was
that my frustration retreated into neglect, while his became a hand.

On the last night before I moved out, Yuzu slept in the hallway. Shen and I passed her on our way
to bed. We stepped around her. We did not say goodnight. The apartment was quiet enough to hear
the refrigerator change cycles. I lay awake and listened to the quiet, and for the first time I
understood that a home can be organized around what nobody is willing to say.

# Epilogue

I moved out in the spring. Shen helped me carry two boxes to the street and stood beside me until
the car came. He asked whether I would need anything else. I said no. He nodded. We did not
embrace. The cat watched from the window and disappeared when the car door opened.

For months I interpreted the year as a story about a difficult friend and a frightened cat. This
was satisfying because it gave me a position outside the household. I was the one who noticed. I
was the one who understood what the slap meant. I was the one who had remained gentle.

The interpretation did not survive contact with my own behaviour.

Shen's silence could be read as withholding, but it was also a practical language. He made food,
watched my moods, planned a route through the city, and placed a bowl on the floor for an animal
he had frightened. His actions did not cancel the violence. They did show me that care can exist
inside a person whose vocabulary for it is narrow and unfinished.

My apparent openness had its own aggression. I gave the cat a child's name and then resented her
for having teeth. I offered Shen warmth and then withdrew when he could not convert it into
speech. I treated observation as innocence. But to watch and not speak is also to let a room
choose its own ending.

Yuzu had been the third member of a household with two people in it. She carried messages,
distributed attention, and made it possible for us to care without naming what we felt. That was
not a failure of the animal. It was an achievement of the animal, and an evasion by the men.

When I remember the apartment now, I do not see a bridge. I see a room with two doors and a cat
walking between them. Each of us believed that the other was on the far side of something. Each of
us waited for the animal to tell us it was safe to cross.

The animal between us was never only the cat.
